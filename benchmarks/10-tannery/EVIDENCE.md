Research, not design. Nothing here picks a colour.

## 3. Materials and surfaces

- **Leather in section.** A hide is a laminate seen edge-on: a dense grain layer at the top
  surface and a looser fibre body beneath it (category). This is why the drawing on this site
  is an edge, not a face — the face is where colour lives and colour is not the difference.
- **Thickness is the material fact.** 0.8 mm to 3.2 mm across the six (verified). The whole
  range is four millimetres, and the two leathers a binder actually weighs against each other
  — 1.2 mm goat, 1.4 mm calf — are two tenths of a millimetre apart (verified, arithmetic).
- **Temper** is how much a hide stands up against how much it drapes (verified as a concept in
  the brief; the six values are verified). It is a hand property, so any drawing of it is a
  notation, and must say so.
- **Grain** is the pattern of the top surface: fine and close, pronounced and irregular,
  coarse, loose and stretchy, smooth (verified per leather).
- **The pit** is a rectangular sunk tank of bark liquor, worked in yards of many pits
  (category). Twenty-eight of them here, nineteen down (verified).
- **Time is a material here.** Nine to fourteen months in bark, against the day a chrome
  tannery takes (verified). Nothing in the process can be shortened, so the calendar is a
  physical property of the product and not a service-level statement.
- Surface implication: rectilinear, ruled, hard-edged. Pit copings, yard bays, a bench, a
  caliper, a ruled page in a yard book. Nothing rounded, nothing floating.

## 6. References and anti-references

**References** — what specifically to take:

1. **A materials data sheet** (a supplier's spec card for a stock material). Take: figures set
   in a monospace, tabular, one row per grade, the unit stated once at the head and never
   repeated in the cells.
2. **An engineering section drawing with a scale bar.** Take: the object drawn edge-on on a
   common baseline, rules at a stated interval, and the honest statement of what the drawing
   is and is not. This is where the plate comes from.
3. **A letterpress type specimen book.** Take: the idea of a *plate* — many specimens of one
   family, set at one size, on one page, so the reader compares by looking rather than by
   remembering. A specimen book never puts two sizes a page apart.

**Anti-references** — what this must not resemble:

1. **The artisan-heritage leather landing page.** Sepia full-bleed photograph of hands and a
   workbench, a serif headline over it, "crafted with care", a newsletter box. It must not
   look like this because it sells feeling to consumers, and this firm sells four-millimetre
   facts to trade buyers who already know what leather is.
2. **The direct-to-consumer leather goods shop.** Big product photography, colour swatches,
   prices, add-to-basket. It must not look like this because colour is explicitly not the
   difference here, there are no prices, and they do not sell to the public.
3. **The default marketing template.** Centred hero over a soft gradient, three equal feature
   cards with round icons, a logo wall, a testimonial. It must not look like this because
   every one of those components would have to be filled with something invented — there are
   no customers who will be named and no certifications to show.

## 8. Two facts that sit oddly together, kept as they are

Recorded here because the design must not smooth them over.

- Goat: **in pit since August 2025**, and **11 months lead from order** at July 2026.
- Sheep: **in pit since February 2026**, and **5 months lead from order** at July 2026.
- Bark tanning takes **9 to 14 months** (verified).

Arithmetic on the verified figures, and nothing more: nine to fourteen months from February
2026 falls between November 2026 and April 2027, and the sheep's five months lands in
December 2026 — inside that window. Nine to fourteen months from August 2025 falls between
May and October 2026, and the goat's eleven months lands in June 2027 — past it.

Any explanation of *why* would be an invention. The calendar therefore plots both facts and
lets the reader see the gap, and the caption says only what the arithmetic says.
