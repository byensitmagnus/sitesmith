Four types account for most of what comes through this practice. Here is what typically fails in each of them, and what a surveyor from here looks at before anything else.

01Mill conversions

Mill conversions

Woollen and worsted mills, mostly nineteenth century, mostly converted between 2000 and 2008 into offices, studios or flats. Solid stone or brick walls, timber or cast iron internal structure, timber or jack-arch floors, and a roof originally designed to be maintained by somebody who worked in the building every day.

The recurring problem is that a solid wall has been asked to behave like a cavity wall. Impermeable render, cement pointing and internal dry lining trap the moisture the wall was built to shed, and it surfaces at the bearing ends of the joists. By the time it is visible internally, the timber has usually been wet for years.

What we look at first

  • Valley gutters, outlets, and the parapet upstands above them
  • Joist and beam bearing ends where they enter the external wall
  • Cement pointing and impermeable render over lime-built masonry
  • Cast iron column bases, and any later steel inserted alongside them
  • Compartmentation between converted units, particularly in the roof void

02Concrete frames

1960s concrete-frame offices

Reinforced concrete frames with infill panels and curtain walling, built when cover to the reinforcement was thinner and less reliably placed than it would be now. A good number have already had one round of concrete repairs.

The question is nearly always whether the deterioration is carbonation or chloride. It matters because the repair specification, the cost and the realistic life afterwards are different in each case — and because patching chloride-contaminated concrete regularly starts new corrosion at the edge of the patch. Testing is cheaper than guessing, and much cheaper than repairing twice.

What we look at first

  • Slab edges, nibs and soffits, where the cover is thinnest
  • Rust staining and cracking following the line of the reinforcement
  • Earlier patch repairs, and what is now happening at their edges
  • Curtain walling gaskets, sealant, and the movement joints
  • The records: previous testing, half-cell survey, or a repair specification

03Retail parks

Retail parks

Single-storey terraces of large-span units, profiled metal or single-ply roofs, and a service yard doing considerably more work than it was specified for. Almost always multi-let, which means the defect and the liability for it are rarely inside the same demise.

Most of what we are called to is water: outlets and gutters at unit boundaries, laps that have opened, rooflights gone brittle, and yard drainage that has been failing slowly since the units were first let. Second is the slab, where the delivery loading changed and nobody went back to check it.

What we look at first

  • Gutters and outlets, especially where a run crosses a demise boundary
  • Sheet laps, fixings, and the condition of the rooflights
  • Where roof responsibility actually sits under each lease, before anything is priced
  • Yard slab joints and drainage under the loading the units take now
  • Fire-stopping at party walls within the roof space

04Listed industrial

Listed industrial

Mills, engine houses, warehouses and workshops that are listed, sit in a conservation area, or are attached to something that is. The building physics is no different from an unlisted one; what changes is that the obvious repair may not be a lawful one.

The failure mode we see most often is well-intentioned maintenance — cement pointing on lime-built masonry, modern sealant at stone joints, and replacement rainwater goods in a material that was cheaper and now sheds water down the face of the wall. Undoing that is slower and dearer than the defect it was meant to fix.

What we look at first

  • Pointing: what is lime, what is cement, and what the cement is doing to the stone
  • Cast iron rainwater goods, and whether anybody can actually reach them
  • Windows and glazing, and what consent a replacement would require
  • The consent history — what was approved, what was built, and whether those agree
  • Whether the work you want is a repair or an alteration, before it is specified
