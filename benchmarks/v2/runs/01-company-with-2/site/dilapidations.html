Dilapidations

Interim and terminal schedules, quantified demands and responses — for landlords and for tenants, though never for both on the same building.

01Sides

Which side we are on, and why we only take one

Dilapidations is adversarial by design. A practice that is comfortable on both sides of the same schedule is not being careful with either of them. We take one side per building, confirm it in writing at the start, and check for conflicts before we accept anything.

  • Acting for the landlord

    Interim schedules while the lease is running, terminal schedules at expiry, and the quantified demand that has to accompany one. Endorsed — because an unendorsed schedule invites the response that the works were never necessary and the costs were never tested.

  • Acting for the tenant

    A response that tests every item in turn: is it a breach at all, is the remedy the cheapest that discharges the covenant, and would the landlord ever have carried it out? Then the section 18(1) question, which is frequently the whole negotiation.

02The cap

Section 18(1) decides more of this than the schedule does

Damages for breach of a repairing covenant cannot exceed the amount by which the value of the landlord's reversion is diminished by the breach. A schedule running to six figures on a building that is about to be stripped back and redeveloped is not a claim for six figures, and both sides are better off knowing that early.

The diminution figure itself is a valuation instruction and needs a valuer. What a building surveyor can do is scope the schedule so that the question is worth asking, flag it while there is still time to run it, and keep the item-level detail in a form the valuer can actually use.

Section 18(1) also cuts off works the landlord has superseded. If the partitions were coming out anyway, their reinstatement is not a loss, and a schedule that ignores that will not survive a response from anyone competent.

03Protocol

What the protocol expects, and when

The pre-action protocol for claims about the physical state of commercial property sets the sequence. It is not optional, and the courts take notice of who followed it.

  • The schedule, endorsed

    The surveyor endorses that in their opinion the works are necessary to remedy the breaches and the costs are reasonable. That is a professional statement with a name against it, which is why some schedules quietly arrive without one.

  • The quantified demand

    Sent with the schedule or shortly after it, setting out the loss claimed, with the landlord confirming whether the works have been done, will be done, or what is actually intended for the property.

  • The response, in time

    Usually taken as 56 days from receipt. A response that arrives late, or arrives item by item without a stated position, hands the initiative to the other side for no reason.

  • A meeting that is worth having

    The protocol expects the surveyors to meet. Turning up with a marked-up Scott Schedule and an agreed list of what is genuinely still in dispute shortens that meeting, and shortens everything after it.

04Method

How we work a schedule

  1. Read the lease and every licence

    Repairing covenant, decoration covenant, yielding-up clause, and every licence for alterations. Then the schedule of condition, if one exists — it is the single document that most often changes the answer.

    You provideLease, licences, any schedule of condition

  2. Inspect and record against the covenant

    Item by item, photographed and referenced, with the other side's surveyor present where that will save an argument later. A joint inspection costs one morning and regularly removes a third of the items.

    You provideAccess, and a date both surveyors can make

  3. Price it at what it would cost

    Costed at what the work would genuinely be procured for, not at a rate set to open a negotiation. A schedule priced to be argued down is one that nobody, including the judge, believes.

    You provideNothing

  4. Negotiate, and write down what is agreed

    Item by item in a Scott Schedule, with the reason recorded against each. Whatever is not agreed then already sits in the form a mediator or a court would want it in.

    You provideInstructions on where you would settle

05Questions

Asked before instructing us

Do you act for tenants as well as landlords?

Yes, and roughly as often. Never on both sides of the same building, and we run a conflict check before we accept anything — including against instructions the practice has held in the past on the same property.

When should a terminal schedule be prepared?

Early enough that the tenant can genuinely price the decision between doing the works and paying for them. Serving at the last moment removes that choice, and removing it rarely helps the landlord once the response arrives.

Is a schedule of condition worth doing at the start of a lease?

Almost always. It is the cheapest document in the whole sequence and it is the one most often missing when we are called in at the end of it. If you are letting a mill conversion or an ageing industrial unit, it is the difference between a negotiation and an argument.

Can you produce the diminution valuation?

No — that is a valuation instruction and it needs a valuer. We will tell you when the point is live and worth the cost, and we will scope and detail the schedule so that the valuation can actually be run off it.

Tell us the building and the lease date.

Which side you are on, what kind of building it is, and when the term ends or the schedule has to be served. If it is not work for us, we will say so on the call rather than take it.
