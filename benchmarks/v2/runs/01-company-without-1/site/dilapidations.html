Dilapidations

Lease ends are mostly decided eighteen months before they happen. We prepare schedules and quantified demands for landlords, and responses for tenants, and we cost both of them the same way.

At a glance

For
Landlords and managing agents at or before lease expiry; tenants planning an exit, a break, or an assignment.
When to start
Landlords: three to six months before expiry. Tenants: twelve to eighteen months, if you want the option of doing works instead of paying for them.
Turnaround
Inspection within ten working days of instruction; a costed schedule usually three weeks after that, depending on the size of the demise.
You receive
A schedule in the standard format with the lease clause, the breach, the remedy and the cost for every item, endorsed as the Protocol requires.
Fee
Fixed for preparation of the schedule; negotiation charged separately at an hourly rate agreed in advance, so you can see what the argument is costing.

If you are the landlord

Interim schedules

Served during the term where disrepair is running away and the lease allows it, often under a Jervis v Harris clause. Worth doing properly — an interim schedule that cannot be enforced is a wasted round of correspondence and hardens the tenant's position for the terminal one.

Terminal schedules and quantified demands

Prepared from the lease, the licences to alter, the schedule of condition if there is one, and an inspection that covers the whole demise including plant and roof. Each item is tied to the clause it breaches, with a remedy and a cost, and the demand is served in the timescale the Protocol expects.

Endorsement

We endorse schedules in the terms the Protocol requires: that the works are those we genuinely consider are required, and that the costs are reasonable. We will not endorse a schedule that has been padded, which occasionally means a conversation before it goes out rather than after.

If you are the tenant

Liability assessment, early

A costed view of exposure twelve to eighteen months out, in a form your finance team can use for a provision. It also tells you whether doing the works yourself is cheaper than settling, which it often is — and that option disappears once the lease ends.

Responses and Scott Schedules

A line-by-line response to the landlord's schedule: what is agreed, what is disputed, and why. Where the matter is heading to proceedings, in Scott Schedule format so nobody has to retype it.

The arguments worth having

Supersession, where the landlord's own works make the repair irrelevant. Scope of the covenant and the standard of repair for the age and character of the building. Betterment in the costings. And whether the claim survives the section 18(1) cap at all.

Section 18(1)

We raise the cap at the start, not at the end

Damages for terminal dilapidations are capped at the loss in value of the landlord's reversion caused by the breaches. If the building is going to be redeveloped, relet after a comprehensive refurbishment, or simply cannot be improved by the works claimed, a large schedule can be worth very little.

We are building surveyors, not valuers, so where the cap looks likely to bite we say so and recommend a diminution valuation from a valuer at the point it will change the decision. Both sides tend to spend less overall when that conversation happens in month one rather than month nine.

A schedule with everything in it is not a strong schedule. It signals to the other side that nothing in it has been thought about, and it makes the items that do matter harder to defend.

How the negotiation usually goes

  1. Schedule and quantified demand served. Endorsed, costed, with the landlord's intentions for the building stated.
  2. Response. Item by item, with the tenant's surveyor's costs and any supersession or section 18(1) points made.
  3. Surveyors meet. Usually on site. Most schedules reduce substantially here, and this is where experience of the building type earns its fee.
  4. Settlement, or a narrowed dispute. Either a figure both sides can justify to their client, or a short list of genuine differences that a court could actually decide.

We act for landlords and for tenants, though never on the same building, and we tell you at the first call if we are already instructed on the other side.
