Service 01 · What we are known for

Defect diagnosis

Something has gone wrong, two or three people have already offered an explanation, and none of them agrees. We work out what is actually happening, say so in writing, and stand behind it.

When people ring us

  • Water is coming in and three contractors have blamed each other. Roof, wall, window, drainage or condensation — all four look identical from inside, and the wrong answer has usually already been paid for once.
  • Cracking has appeared and nobody can say whether it matters. Movement, thermal, settlement, sulfate attack, or simply the building doing what it has always done. The distinction decides whether you spend £4,000 or £400,000.
  • Concrete is spalling on a 1960s frame. Carbonation, chlorides, cover depth. Whether the programme is patch repair, cathodic protection or something more serious, and how urgent it really is.
  • A tenant and a landlord disagree about disrepair. Whether the damage is disrepair, inherent defect, design fault or fair wear and tear — and therefore whose cost it is.
  • A new-build or refurbishment is not performing. Early failures where the contract administrator, the designer and the contractor each have a version of events and a limitation clock running.

How the work runs

  1. A call before anything else

    Twenty minutes on the phone tells us whether this is a half-day inspection or a six-month monitoring exercise. Sometimes it tells us you do not need us at all, and we will say that too.

  2. The record, before the building

    Drawings, specification, O&M manuals, previous reports, repair history, correspondence. Half of all defect work is understanding what was intended before you look at what was built.

  3. Inspection

    On site, at height and at depth where it is needed — access equipment, drone or rope access arranged as required. Moisture readings, thermography, cover meter and carbonation testing where they will actually distinguish between competing explanations rather than just add pages.

  4. Opening up, or monitoring

    Some mechanisms cannot be settled by looking. Where a hypothesis needs an opening-up or a period of crack or moisture monitoring to be tested properly, we will tell you the cost and the timescale before you commit, not after.

  5. The report

    Findings, mechanism, options with budget costs, and a clear statement of what the evidence does and does not support on cause and responsibility. Usually within ten working days of the last site visit.

  6. Afterwards

    The surveyor who wrote it takes the calls about it, sits in the meeting with the other side if you want them there, and reviews the remedial design if you ask. No handover to somebody who has never seen the building.

What you get

  • A single-page summary at the front for the people who will not read forty pages.
  • Observed condition, set out room by room or elevation by elevation, referenced to a plan so a third party can find what we found.
  • Diagnosis of the mechanism, with the alternatives we considered and why we discounted them. If two explanations remain live, we say so.
  • Remedial options with budget costs and consequences — including the option of doing nothing yet, where that is genuinely defensible.
  • An explicit statement of limitations: what was not accessible, what was not opened up, and what further investigation would resolve.
  • Photographic record, dated, with instrument readings recorded as taken.

On apportioning blame

We will say what the evidence supports about cause. Whether that translates into liability is a legal question and we do not pretend otherwise. What we can do is make sure the technical part of the argument is solid enough that the legal part can be had properly — and that if it becomes formal, the report is already in a shape your solicitor can work with.

What we need from you

  • The building type and address, and how old it is if you know.
  • What has gone wrong, in your own words. “It leaks when the wind is from the west” is more useful than a technical guess.
  • Anything already written about it — previous reports, contractor quotes, emails. Including the ones you think are wrong.
  • Whether a dispute is live or looks likely, and whether anybody has a deadline.
  • Access arrangements and who holds the keys.
