Reference · Building types

Buildings we know

Around 180 instructions a year, most of them on four or five kinds of building. Every type has its own catalogue of failures, and knowing the catalogue is most of the job.

01 Mill conversions

Stone, cast iron, and forty years of well-meant repairs.

West Yorkshire has more converted mills per square mile than anywhere else in the country, and they are among the most rewarding buildings to work on and the easiest to get wrong.

  • Breathability. Solid walls that were designed to take up moisture and give it back, sealed with cement render, impermeable paint or an unventilated dry lining. The damp then appears somewhere else and gets diagnosed as rising damp, treated chemically, and comes back.
  • Cast iron and timber frames. Beam-end decay at wall pockets, corrosion at bearing points, and columns that were never designed for the loads a conversion imposes. Usually hidden by the fit-out.
  • Roof and rainwater geometry. Long valleys, box gutters and internal downpipes carrying more water than anything modern, generally with no overflow and no access for cleaning.
  • Stone and pointing. Hard cement pointing on soft sandstone, spalled faces, delaminating ashlar, and lintels and cills doing more work than they were asked to.
  • The conversion itself. Compartmentation, means of escape and acoustic separation done to the standards of the year of conversion, not of today.

02 1960s concrete-frame offices

Sixty years into the design life of something that was designed for sixty years.

These are the buildings where the difference between a competent survey and a quick one shows up most sharply, because almost everything that matters is behind something else.

  • Reinforcement corrosion. Carbonation front against cover depth, chloride ingress where de-icing salts or coastal exposure apply, and the spalling and rust staining that follows. Cover meter and carbonation testing separate a patch repair from a programme.
  • Deleterious materials. High-alumina cement in the right vintages, RAAC planks, woodwool permanent shuttering, calcium silicate brick, asbestos in almost every form the industry produced.
  • Cladding and its fixings. Mosaic, tile and stone facings losing adhesion; brick slips; curtain walling with failed gaskets and no thermal break; and fixings that were never designed to be inspected.
  • Movement joints. Frequently omitted, frequently filled, and a common source of cracking that gets attributed to settlement.
  • Refurbishment archaeology. Three or four fit-outs, each of which cut something structural or fire-related and none of which is fully recorded.

03 Retail parks and distribution

Big, simple buildings that fail in a small number of predictable places.

Portal-frame sheds are straightforward until they are not. The failures cluster around water, movement and whatever the last tenant did to the shell.

  • Roofs and gutters. Single-ply and profiled metal laid to shallow falls, valley gutters sized against historic rainfall data, blocked or undersized outlets, and no overflow provision. One blocked outlet and a summer storm is a six-figure interruption claim.
  • Roof-mounted plant. Added over the years without structural checks, penetrations sealed by whoever installed the unit, and access routes that damage the covering every time anybody services it.
  • Cladding and rooflights. Composite panel delamination, corrosion at cut edges and fixings, and GRP rooflights that are long past both their light transmission and their fragility ratings.
  • Slabs and service yards. Cracking, settlement at dock levellers, and hardstanding degraded by loads it was never designed for.
  • Demise and fit-out. What is actually the tenant's, what the shell specification said, and what has been cut through the fire compartmentation between units.

04 Listed industrial

The building, the legislation and the conservation officer all have a view.

Listed industrial buildings need the same technical diagnosis as anything else, plus a repair strategy that will actually get consent. Getting those in the wrong order wastes months.

  • Record before intervention. Photographic and drawn record of what is there now, so that later argument about what was lost has an answer.
  • Repair in kind. Lime rather than cement, matched stone and mortar specification, and repair rather than replacement wherever the fabric allows it.
  • Consent strategy. Early engagement with the conservation officer, with the options and their justification set out rather than presented as a single proposal to approve or refuse.
  • Compliance against fabric. Fire, thermal and accessibility upgrades that are proportionate and reversible, and an honest account of where full compliance is not achievable.
  • Ongoing decay. Water is the whole problem. Gutters, downpipes, roof coverings and ventilation, maintained on a cycle rather than fixed in crises.
