Service 02 · Commercial acquisition

Pre-purchase building surveys

A survey for people who are buying a building rather than a home. What you are taking on, what it will cost you over the next ten years, and which of those numbers ought to change the price or the contract.

Most acquisition surveys we are asked to review have the same fault: they describe the building accurately and then leave the reader to work out what to do about it. A property manager with three days before a board paper is due does not need a catalogue. They need to know which items are deal-relevant, what they cost, and how confident anybody can be about the number.

So we write the summary for the deal first, and the technical record behind it. The record still has to be complete — it is the thing that has to hold up later — but it is not the thing you read at 9pm the night before exchange.

What the survey covers

  • Structure. Frame, floors, foundations by inference, evidence of movement, load paths where alterations have been made. Where a structural engineer is genuinely needed we say so early rather than hedge for forty pages.
  • Envelope. Roof coverings and drainage, walls, cladding and its fixings, windows, sealants, thermal performance and the interfaces where most failures actually start.
  • Services. Age, condition and remaining life of plant by visual inspection and record review, with specialist M&E testing commissioned where the value of the plant justifies it. We do not pretend a visual inspection is a condition survey of a chiller.
  • Deleterious and hazardous materials. Asbestos register and management survey review, high-alumina cement, RAAC in the right vintages, woodwool, calcium silicate brick. Identified, and flagged for the right specialist.
  • Statutory and compliance observations. Means of escape, compartmentation as observed, accessibility, EPC and MEES exposure, and anything that will bite at the next letting rather than at completion.
  • Occupation and demise. Where the building is let, what the leases assume about repair, and whether the physical building matches the plans that were drawn.

What you get

  • A red-flag summary on page one. The three to six things that could change your view of the transaction, with figures.
  • A costed schedule over one, five and ten years. Budget costs at current rates, separated into immediate, short-term and lifecycle, so it can go straight into a cashflow.
  • A confidence rating against each cost. Some numbers are firm. Some are an allowance pending opening up. Pretending they are the same standard of certainty helps nobody.
  • Photographic and drawn record referenced so that the surveyor acting for the other side can find exactly what we are describing.
  • A stated basis and scope — what was inspected, what was not, and why. Reliance and any assignment arrangements agreed before we start, not after.

Timing

  1. Instruction and access

    Access is almost always the constraint, not us. Tell us the date you need the report by and we will work backwards and tell you honestly whether it is achievable.

  2. Inspection

    Typically within five to ten working days of instruction. A single let office or a mid-size industrial unit is usually a day; a multi-let estate is not.

  3. Report

    Normally ten working days from the last visit. If your timetable is tighter than that, say so at the outset — sometimes we can issue the red-flag summary first and the full report behind it.

What this is not

It is not a valuation — that is a valuer's job and we will tell you when you need one. It is not a measured survey or a full M&E condition survey, though we will tell you when either is worth commissioning. And it is not a residential homebuyer report. We do not do those at all.

Working to a deadline?

Give us the building, the town and your exchange or decision date. We will tell you within the hour whether we can hit it.
