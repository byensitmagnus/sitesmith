Service 01 — the work we are known for

Defect diagnosis

Something has gone wrong, the parties disagree about why, and the answer needs to hold when it is challenged. We establish the cause from the building and write it so it can be relied on.

Talk to a surveyor

§ 01When you call

When people call us about a defect

  • Water is coming in and the roofing contractor, the main contractor and the warranty provider have each explained why it is not theirs
  • A concrete frame is spalling and nobody can say whether it is a maintenance problem or a structural one
  • Cracking has appeared and the argument is about whether it is historic movement or something happening now
  • A fit-out has gone in and the building has behaved differently since
  • An expert has produced a report for the other side and somebody needs to say whether it stands up

What these have in common is not the defect. It is that the cause is contested and the answer will be read by people looking for the weak point in it.

§ 02Method

What the work involves

The order matters. Opening up a building before you have read what was built is how you destroy the evidence you came for.

  1. The record first

    Drawings, specification, the construction record, previous reports, the maintenance history and the correspondence. Often the defect is already described in a document somebody stopped reading.

  2. Inspection

    The whole building, not the complaint. A failure at one elevation is frequently the visible end of something general.

  3. Opening up, where the evidence is buried

    Agreed with you in advance, in the fewest positions that will settle the question, with reinstatement specified. We say what each opening is expected to show, before it is made.

  4. Monitoring, where the movement is not yet understood

    Some causes cannot be established from one visit. Where that is the case we say so and set out what monitoring would establish, over what period, at what cost — rather than guessing to fill the gap.

  5. Specialist testing where it is needed

    Laboratory analysis, structural engineering input, mechanical and electrical investigation. We bring in the right discipline rather than stretching ours.

  6. The report

    What the building is, what has happened to it, why, and what follows from that. Findings and opinion kept visibly apart, so a reader can see which is which.

§ 03The report

What you get, and who it is written for

The report is written for the next person to read it, which is rarely you. It is usually a solicitor, sometimes an adjudicator, occasionally an expert on the other side whose job is to find the gap in it.

  • The evidence set out before the conclusion, so the conclusion can be tested against it
  • Photographs referenced to positions on a plan, not a gallery at the back
  • Findings separated from opinion, and opinion separated from what we would do about it
  • Limitations stated: what was not inspected, what could not be opened up, and what that means for the certainty of the conclusion
  • A written scope agreed before we start, so the report answers the question you asked

“Every report we write can end up in front of a court, so it has to be defensible. We would rather turn down work than write something we cannot stand behind.”

The practice's own position

In practice that means we will tell you when the evidence does not support the conclusion you were hoping for, and we will tell you before you have spent money finding out.

§ 04Limits

What we will not do

  • Write a conclusion the evidence does not support, however firmly the instruction is worded
  • Present a probable cause as an established one — where it cannot be determined without opening up, the report says so and says what it would take
  • Act for both sides of the same dispute
  • Take an instruction where the deadline does not allow the investigation the question needs. We would rather say no on the call
  • Residential homebuyer reports, in any circumstances
