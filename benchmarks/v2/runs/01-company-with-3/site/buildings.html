Four building types — what we look for in each

Buildings we know

If you were given our name and you are checking whether we have done this kind of building before, this is the page for it. Four types account for most of our year, across all three services. Each fails in its own way.

Talk to a surveyor

§ 01Mill conversions

Mill conversions

A nineteenth-century mill converted to offices or apartments is two buildings: the one that was built and the one that was inserted into it. Most of the disputes we see happen at the join.

  • Frame — cast iron, wrought iron or timber, with jack-arch or timber floors. Notching and drilling during the conversion, for services that had to go somewhere, is a recurring find
  • Walls — solid stone or brick with no cavity, built to breathe and now expected to stay dry against a modern lease. Rising and penetrating damp usually turns out to be a detail at ground level or a blocked gutter, not the wall itself
  • Impermeable repairs — cement pointing and modern renders on walls that need lime. A frequent cause of damp that was introduced by the last attempt to fix damp
  • Compartmentation — where the conversion divided a single open volume, fire stopping at floor and party lines is the thing to check and the thing least likely to have been recorded
  • Sound between units — heavy floors, light partitions, and a complaint history that arrives before the defect does
  • Roofs and rainwater — long valleys, hidden gutters, and rooflights added at conversion into a covering that was never designed for them

§ 02Concrete frames

1960s and 1970s concrete-frame offices

These buildings are now sixty years into a design life nobody wrote down. They fail slowly and then all at once, and the question is almost always whether what you are looking at is maintenance or structure.

  • Carbonation and cover — where the concrete cover to the reinforcement is shallow, carbonation reaches the steel, the steel corrodes and expands, and the concrete spalls. Cover meter readings and carbonation depth testing turn an argument into a number
  • Chlorides — where they are present, from the mix or from de-icing salts at deck and podium level, the repair strategy is a different one and considerably more expensive
  • Spalling at edges and soffits — usually first visible at exposed slab edges, balconies and car park soffits, which is where somebody eventually gets hit by a piece of it
  • Curtain walling — original gaskets and sealants thirty years past their service life, single glazing, thermal bridging at the transoms and a water path nobody can trace from the inside
  • Asbestos — likely in the fabric of buildings of this age; the register and the survey behind it are the first documents we ask for
  • Previous repairs — patch repairs in a stronger mortar than the parent concrete drive the corrosion to the edge of the patch. Rows of neat square repairs are a symptom, not a cure

§ 03Retail parks

Retail parks and trade units

Simple buildings with expensive failures. A retail unit is a steel frame, a skin and a drainage strategy, and almost everything that goes wrong goes wrong at the third one.

  • Valley and eaves gutters — the single largest source of terminal schedule value we see on these units. Corroded, undersized, overflowing internally, and repaired with a liner that has now failed as well
  • Profiled cladding and roof sheets — cut-edge corrosion, failed fixings and washers, and coating life that expired quietly during the second tenant's term
  • Rooflights — brittle with age, a safety issue for anyone accessing the roof, and a recurring point of water ingress
  • Frame and portal — usually sound, but check where a tenant has hung services, mezzanines or racking from it without a licence
  • Sub-division and fit-out — units split and re-split between lettings, with compartmentation, drainage and yield-up obligations that no longer match the plan attached to the lease
  • Service yards and paving — settlement, standing water, and drainage under a surface that HGVs use daily. Frequently excluded from the schedule and frequently the biggest number in it

§ 04Listed industrial

Listed industrial buildings

Here the repair has to satisfy the conservation officer as well as the building, and the cheapest technical answer is often not an available one. The constraint is not a formality — getting it wrong is an offence, and it delays everything.

  • Consent — what needs listed building consent, what does not, and what the local authority will want to see before it decides. Established before the specification is written, not after
  • Like-for-like — matching mortar, stone, brick and detail because the consent requires it, which changes the cost, the programme and the trades available
  • Lime — mortars and renders specified to suit the wall rather than the programme. Cement on a solid wall is the mistake that generates the next instruction
  • Ironwork and glazing — original windows, rooflights and structural iron, where replacement is rarely consentable and repair is a specialist trade with a lead time
  • Dilapidations under a listing — a schedule that requires works the tenant cannot lawfully carry out is not a schedule. This has to be worked through at drafting, on both sides

§ 05Anything else

If your building is not one of these

These four are where most of our year goes, not a list of what we will accept. We work across commercial property generally in Yorkshire and the North East. Ring and describe the building; if it is outside what we should be doing, we will say so on the call.

Not residential

We do not carry out residential homebuyer reports — RICS Home Survey Level 1, 2 or 3 — whatever the building type. That has been the position since 2004.

Tell us the building and the deadline.

The type, the age, roughly the size and the date. That is enough for us to say whether it is work we should take.
