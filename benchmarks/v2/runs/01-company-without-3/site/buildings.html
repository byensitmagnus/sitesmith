Buildings

Have you done a building like mine before?

It is the second question everyone asks and the one a list of services never answers. We cannot publish client names — we have not asked permission, and we are not going to assume it — so here is the honest version: the building types that make up most of our year, and what we go looking for in each of them.

Four types, roughly three quarters of our instructions

Mill conversions

Stone or brick, mostly 1850 to 1900, four to six storeys, with a cast-iron or timber internal frame. Converted to offices or apartments in the wave that ran from the late nineties to 2008, which means they are now twenty-odd years into their second life and the conversion detailing is reaching the age at which it starts to fail.

Nearly every instruction is about water. The roof gets blamed first and is very often not the culprit.

  • Parapet and hidden valley gutters — the usual actual source, and the hardest to inspect
  • Hard cement repointing trapping moisture in soft stone and accelerating decay
  • Timber beam ends and cast-iron bearings buried in damp external walls behind conversion-era linings
  • Cold bridging and interstitial condensation from the conversion, routinely blamed on how occupants use the space
  • Deep reveals, early replacement windows and the junctions around them

1960s and 1970s concrete-frame offices

The type people are most nervous about, and the one where the diagnosis moves the number most. Spalling at a slab edge can be a cosmetic repair or the visible end of widespread reinforcement corrosion, and the difference between those two answers is the difference between a maintenance line and a redevelopment appraisal.

Getting it right takes testing rather than opinion, which is why we push for half-cell and carbonation work before anyone commits to a figure.

  • Carbonation and chloride-induced corrosion of embedded reinforcement; spalling at soffits, edges and copings
  • Earlier patch repairs driving incipient anode corrosion in the sound concrete around them
  • Movement joints that were never provided, or have been sealed shut by later works
  • Mosaic, tile and applied finishes debonding at height
  • Curtain walling, gaskets and single-glazed units at the end of their life
  • Reinforced autoclaved aerated concrete in roof planks, which we check for explicitly on stock of this period
  • Frame capacity where a change of use or new roof plant is proposed

Retail parks

Cheap to build, expensive to argue about. Nearly always multi-let, which means every defect immediately becomes a service charge question as well as a repair question, and the tenant who reported it is usually the one being asked to pay for it.

Roofs and yards account for most of the work. Tenant fit-outs account for most of the surprises.

  • Single-ply and built-up roof coverings at or beyond design life, with patch repairs on patch repairs
  • Interstitial condensation presented as a leak, or blamed on the tenant's use of the unit
  • Roof-level plant added during fit-out, with penetrations detailed by whoever was on site that day
  • Rainwater outlets and siphonic drainage, and what happens internally when they block
  • Service yards, slabs, kerbs and drainage — unglamorous and frequently the largest single figure in the report
  • Compartmentation broken through by fit-out works

Listed and historic industrial

Weaving sheds, engine houses, warehouses and bonded stores. The technical problem is often the easy half. The remedy also has to be one a conservation officer will accept, which rules out a good deal of what a modern contractor would propose by default.

We would rather have that conversation with the local authority early than design a repair twice.

  • North-light roofs: glazing, cast-iron valley gutters and the beams they sit on
  • Cement repairs to lime-built fabric, which regularly cause the decay they were meant to arrest
  • Cast and wrought iron — corrosion at bearings, jack-arch floors, and columns cased in later material
  • Rainwater disposal designed around a process the building no longer carries out
  • Fire strategy and escape in buildings that will not accept standard solutions
  • What needs listed building consent, and how long the authority will realistically take

05 — Everything else

If your building is not one of those four.

The rest of the year is distribution and light industrial sheds, multi-let town centre offices, hotels and licensed premises, schools and other public buildings, and the occasional car park — which is really a concrete-frame instruction wearing a different hat.

If it is genuinely outside what we do, we will say so on the phone. That happens a few times a year and nobody has ever minded being told.
