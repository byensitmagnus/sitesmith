Service 01 — the one we are known for

Defect diagnosis

A building is doing something it should not. There is a contractor, a developer, a landlord, a tenant and an insurer, and each of them has a different account of why. We work out what is actually happening, and write it down in a form that survives being argued with.

01 — When we are called

The call usually comes late.

Rarely at the first sign. Normally after a contractor has been back twice, after a repair has not worked, or after somebody's solicitor has used the word “liability”. By then there is a history: previous reports, part-completed remedial work, and at least two people confident they already know the answer.

That history matters. Half of a diagnosis is reading what has already been done and working out which of the earlier conclusions the evidence actually supports. We would rather see the whole file, including the reports that disagree with each other.

Typical instructions

  • Persistent water ingress in a converted mill where nobody can agree whether it is the roof, the parapet, the pointing or the windows.
  • Cracking in a 1960s concrete-frame office — corrosion of embedded reinforcement, thermal movement, or foundation movement, and the answer changes the cost by an order of magnitude.
  • Failing single-ply or built-up roofs on retail and industrial units, usually with interstitial condensation being blamed on the tenant's use.
  • Movement and decay in listed industrial buildings, where the remedy has to satisfy a conservation officer as well as a structural argument.
  • Cladding, curtain walling and rainscreen failures on 1990s and 2000s stock.
  • Defects appearing inside a building contract's defects liability period, where the question is whether it is workmanship, design or specification.

02 — Method

Evidence first, opinion second, and the two kept apart.

The order matters. A report that mixes what was observed with what it means is very hard for anyone else to test, and a report nobody can test is worth very little when it is challenged.

  1. Desk review before the visit

    Drawings, specifications, the building contract if there is one, previous reports, maintenance records, and the correspondence. We ask for the awkward documents too.

  2. Inspection

    Usually a full day, often more than one visit and frequently in wet weather, because a water ingress defect that has stopped is a defect you cannot see. Roof level, voids, plant areas and the affected interiors, with moisture readings, thermal imaging and photographs referenced to a plan.

  3. Opening up, where the evidence needs it

    Targeted, agreed in advance with a cost, and reinstated. A small number of well-chosen openings usually settles an argument that another year of speculation would not.

  4. Monitoring and testing where the answer needs time

    Crack monitoring over months, water testing to a hose test regime, half-cell potential surveys and carbonation testing on reinforced concrete, core sampling. Commissioned from specialists, interpreted by us.

  5. Report

    Observed condition. Then the cause, with the evidence for it and the alternatives we considered and rejected. Then the remedy, its indicative cost, and what happens if it is not done. Then, plainly, the limits of what we inspected.

03 — What you get

The report is the product.

Cause
Stated as a conclusion, with the level of confidence made explicit. Where two mechanisms are both contributing we say so and apportion as far as the evidence allows, rather than picking the tidier answer.
Evidence
Photographs numbered and located on a plan, readings tabulated, test results appended in full rather than summarised into invisibility.
Remedy
What needs doing, in what order, with indicative costs and a note on which elements need a specialist contractor or a designer's involvement.
Consequences of doing nothing
Because that is usually one of the options on the table, and it deserves to be priced honestly rather than used as a threat.
Limitations
What was not accessible, what was not opened up, what was assumed. Stated at the front, not buried in an appendix.
Expert form, where instructed
Where the instruction is for proceedings, the report is prepared in the form required by Part 35 of the Civil Procedure Rules, with the statement of truth and the declaration of the duty to the court. Our duty is to the court, and that does not change because of who is paying.

We will not write a conclusion the evidence does not support. If what you need is a report that says a particular party is at fault, and the building does not say that, we are the wrong practice for the job and we will tell you at the enquiry stage.

04 — Practicalities

Timescale
Inspection usually within five to ten working days of instruction, report within a fortnight of the last visit. Where monitoring is required, an interim note first so you are not waiting in silence.
Fees
Fixed where the scope can be fixed. Where the cause is genuinely unknown at the outset we quote a first stage with a defined end point, and you decide whether to go further.
Who attends
One of the four chartered surveyors, named in the fee proposal. The person who inspects is the person who writes it and the person who defends it.
Coverage
Leeds and the whole of Yorkshire as standard. Regularly across the North East — Teesside, Tyne and Wear, Durham and Northumberland.
