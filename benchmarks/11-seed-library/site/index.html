Take up to six packets of seed. In autumn, bring some back.

A lending library of vegetable seed inside Cleeve Branch Library, Gloucestershire, run by four volunteers since 2019.

The seed is free and it stays free. What keeps the drawers full is the second half: in autumn you save seed from your best plants and bring some of it back. There is no deposit and no penalty if you do not — which is why this page spends its length explaining the return instead of enforcing it.

What is in the drawers, and which of it you should take

Some crops keep themselves to themselves and give you back the plant you grew. Others cross with whatever is flowering down the lane, and the seed you save will be a surprise. Take the first kind in your first year.

How to read the drawings

Drawn, not photographed — nobody has ever photographed these drawers, and we would rather draw the seed than borrow a picture of somebody else’s. Solid means the seed you save grows the plant you took it from. A broken outline means it will not. That is the only difference the drawings carry, it is the only one you need on the way in, and it is also roughly what you will be looking at in autumn with the seed out of the pod and in your hand.

Why four of them cross

The difference is not the plant, and it is not how hard the plant is to grow. It is where the pollen came from, and how far it travelled to get there.

The nine drawers as at July 2026

  • Runner bean ‘Czar’

    Self-pollinating, and the seed is obvious in the pod.

    61 packets

    In autumn Leave some pods on the plant until they are brown and rattle. Shell them, dry the beans until they are hard, bring a few back.

  • Pea ‘Champion of England’

    Self-pollinating.

    44 packets

    In autumn The same as the beans. Let the pods go papery on the plant, shell them, dry the peas until they are hard.

  • Tomato ‘Ailsa Craig’

    Needs fermenting, which is one extra step.

    38 packets

    In autumn Squeeze the seed and its jelly into a jar of water and leave it three or four days until the jelly separates. Rinse, dry flat, and that is the extra step.

  • Lettuce ‘Bronze Arrow’

    Bolts and sets seed readily.

    29 packets

    In autumn Let one plant bolt and flower instead of eating it. When the heads go fluffy, rub them out over a bowl.

  • French bean ‘Cherokee Trail of Tears’

    Self-pollinating.

    22 packets

    In autumn As the runner bean. Dry pods on the plant, hard seed, a few back in the drawer.

  • Kale ‘Pentland Brig’

    Crosses with any brassica within a mile.

    14 packets

    In autumn Only worth saving if you can keep it a mile from any other brassica in flower. Otherwise grow it, eat it, and take a bean home instead.

  • Squash ‘Crown Prince’

    Crosses with any other C. maxima.

    9 packets

    In autumn Only worth saving if no other C. maxima is flowering nearby. Otherwise the seed you bring back grows something nobody planted.

  • Beetroot ‘Cheltenham Green Top’

    Wind-pollinated, needs isolation.

    7 packets

    In autumn Wind-pollinated, so it needs real distance from anything else in flower. Not a first-year crop to save.

  • Carrot ‘Autumn King’

    Biennial, and crosses with wild carrot.

    4 packets

    In autumn Biennial: it will not flower until its second year, and by then the wild carrot in the hedge has crossed it.

Counted as at July 2026. There is no budget to buy more, so when a drawer is empty it stays empty until somebody brings seed back.

Nothing on it yet. Add up to six packets from the drawers — and if this is your first year, take them from the five that come back true.

Nothing is sent anywhere. This is a list to bring with you — write it down, or keep this page open. The packets are in the drawers, and the drawers are open on a Tuesday and a Saturday.

What you are promising, in six months

What it costs
Nothing, at any point. No membership fee, no deposit, no charge for the packets.
What happens if you bring nothing back
Nothing. There is no penalty and nothing to pay, and you can borrow again next spring.
What we are actually asking for
Seed saved from your best plants — the ones that cropped well and did not bolt or sulk — brought in during autumn opening hours, dry, in a paper envelope or a folded piece of paper with the crop written on it.
How much
Enough to fill a packet or two. Not the whole harvest. One good plant of a bean or a pea is more seed than the drawer can hold.

Ninety-six of four hundred and twelve

We think that is low, and we would rather print it than round it up.

A seed library that only lends is a free shop, and a free shop runs out. The four of us can keep the drawers labelled and the sessions open; we cannot keep them full. Only the 412 can do that, and last year 96 did.

So if you take six packets this week, the useful thing you can do is not to feel guilty in October. It is to take five easy ones and one you are curious about, and save seed from two.

Who runs it, and who has said so

Four volunteers

Two are retired seed-savers. Two are library staff giving their own time. That is the whole organisation.

The library has no funding and takes no donations. The branch provides the drawers and the space, and that is the entire cost base.

Two letters, March 2026

Two Gloucestershire allotment associations wrote to endorse the library and agreed to be named:

  • Bishop’s Cleeve Allotment Society
  • Woodmancote Growers Association

Both letters are on file. Neither association has a badge on this page, because neither was asked for one — they lent their names, and a name belongs in type.
