## The winner: A — Rullen

The page is made of the same material as the product. A drawn band of punched roll paper runs
the full height of the page, the text column sits against it, and the whole thing is set in
warm buff and ink with one felt red. The mechanism is answered by a labelled cross-section
further down, in the same ink on the same paper.

**The signature, in one line:** a full-height band of punched music-roll paper down the left of
the page, turning into a punched strip across the top on a phone, with the section numerals
set in the same slot rhythm beside each heading.

That is the thing a visitor would recognise with the wordmark removed, and it is the one
element that cannot be lifted onto another trade's site. A plumber cannot use a perforated
roll. A piano *tuner* cannot either — this is specifically the workshop that cuts rolls.

**The graft:** the numbered cross-section plate is taken from comp B, redrawn as ink on the
paper ground instead of chalk on near-black, and reduced from a full-width plate to one column
beside the five disclosures. B's numbering convention comes with it: the numerals on the
drawing are the numerals on the list. Recorded here because an unrecorded graft is how three
directions quietly become one.

## Why B lost

**What it did well:** the framed cross-section is the strongest single piece of explanation any
of the three produced, and putting it *before* the headline is a real argument — the customer's
question is mechanical, so answer it with the mechanism. The brass numerals tying drawing to
list is a device worth keeping, and it was kept.

**Why it lost, specifically:** the near-black ground is wrong for who is reading. This page is
opened by a person in their sixties standing in a daylit living room next to the instrument
they have just inherited, and the one thing they must be able to read — 1.850 kr., a half day,
a phone number — sits as brass-on-black in a band at the bottom. Beyond legibility, the dark
plate with capitals and wide tracking reads as an engineering consultancy. Two people who take
four instruments at a time are not that, and the page should not promise it. It also scores
worst on anti-reference 3: a boxed object on a dark field is auction-house treatment.

## Why C lost

**What it did well:** the headline is the best sentence of the three, because "we will not say
what it costs before we have seen it" is a statement only this workshop could make, and setting
it at poster size makes the honesty the offer. The right-hand practical column is a better
piece of information design than anything in A — everything a caller needs, in one place, at
the height of the eye.

**Why it lost, specifically:** with no drawing at all, the five failure names stay a vocabulary
list. The visitor's question is "can mine be saved", and five unfamiliar words with no place to
sit answer it with more uncertainty than they arrived with. Marketing failure mode 1 exactly: a
strong opening over an argument that was never made. It is also the most rebadgeable of the
three — swap the sentence and the same stone-green sheet is a solicitor, a bookbinder or a
boat-builder.

**Not grafted, and worth saying so:** the right-hand practical column was tempting and was left
alone. A rails-both-sides page at 1440 has nowhere for the drawing to go, and the drawing is the
load-bearing asset.
