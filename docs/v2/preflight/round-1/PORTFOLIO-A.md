## 1. One studio applying one template, or three pieces of work?

One studio, one method, and — between two of the three — very nearly one template.

Evidence for one hand, all of it visible on the sheets:

- **The same footer confession, phrased the same way.** B4: "a fictional foundry, built as a design pilot", then a clause separating the invented from the real ("Weights and notes shown are invented and are not those of any real ring"). K7: "A fictional brewery, built as a design pilot… the duty rules they follow are not." R9: "A fictional merchant, built as a design pilot." Same sentence, three trades.
- **No photography anywhere.** Every image on all three pages is a line drawing built for the subject: a bell section with the five partials labelled, a cask outline drawn to cask size, four rope cross-sections drawn to construction. One treatment, one stroke weight per page, no stock.
- **Letterspaced caps for every label and column header** on all three, over hairline rules, on a single flat ground. No gradients, no shadows, no rounded cards, no testimonial band, no CTA band, on any of them.
- **Trade units carry the content** rather than adjectives: cwt/qr and cents on B4, gyle/kilderkin/firkin on K7, mm/t/% per metre on R9.
- **A written empty state** in the closing panel of each: "Nothing booked in yet this week", "Nothing cut yet. Each length you add appears here…", and B4's equivalent, a form intro that says what happens after you send.

Evidence against one template: the three grounds and type systems are genuinely different decisions, not a rotated accent — foundry black with brass and a display serif; cellar navy with a UI sans and traffic-light pills; oatmeal paper with mono figures and a single oxide red used exactly once. A template would not have produced three different first screens this far apart in register.

## 2. Which two are closest, and on what?

**K7 and R9, on layout and row anatomy** — they are the same page twice with a different palette. Both open with a header strap carrying a right-aligned running total (`ORDER 0 CUTS £0.00` / `15 on trade`); both follow with one sentence stating the rule of the place; both then set a letterspaced-caps section label that includes a count (`4 OF 4 CONSIGNMENTS · 15 CASKS` / `STOCK, BY CONSTRUCTION — 5 OF 61 LINES`); both then run a hairline-ruled table whose left column is a line drawing, whose second column is a bold name over a one-line note in grey, whose middle is mono figures under mono caps headers, and whose right end is a single small button per row (`Book in` / `Cut a length`); both close with a secondary panel holding a written empty state, then a small mono disclaimer. Even the failure is shared in kind: both break at 390px in the same place, the illustrated left column.

B4 is the outlier of the three, and only on layout — it is prose-led and two-column rather than tabular. It still shares the ground-plus-caps-labels-plus-hairline-rules grammar and the bespoke line diagram.

## 3. What all three share

A documentary register: each page is built to be *checked* rather than admired. The claim is always a measurement — five partials before and after with the kilograms removed, days late, minimum breaking load per metre — and the imagery is a schematic of the thing being measured rather than a picture of it. Each page also volunteers the thing a seller would normally leave out: B4 has a whole "WHAT WE WILL NOT DO" section and publishes the before figures as well as the after, R9 sets "They are not safe working loads" in the one red on the page and says cut lengths cannot be returned, K7 names the account that is three days late and says nothing has been booked in yet this week. That refusal to flatter is the strongest thing the three have in common and it is rare.

The second shared trait is less flattering: **all three spend their craft on desktop and let mobile take the damage.** B4 pushes its brand and headline below a full-height diagram at 390px, K7 overlaps every row's text with its own illustration, R9 overflows its header strap out of both edges of the viewport. Three pages, three different mobile failures, one shared habit — the narrow width was checked last, if at all.
