## 1. One studio applying one template, or three different pieces of work?

One studio, one method, three different layouts. Not a template — but a recipe, and the recipe is
visible enough that I could pick a fourth page out of a lineup.

**Evidence that it is one hand:**

- **The footer sentence is the same sentence.** All three close with a small note in the same
  position, at the same size, in the same voice: "a fictional foundry, built as a design pilot",
  "A fictional brewery, built as a design pilot", "A fictional merchant, built as a design pilot".
  Each is then followed by a second, trade-specific caveat in the same slot — invented weights,
  invented gyles, breaking loads that are not safe working loads. Same construction, same rhythm,
  three times.
- **Monospace carries every number on all three pages.** B4's dimension callouts and cents table,
  K7's gyle codes, timestamps and "3 d late", R9's entire figure system including the price and
  the "/m". None of the three lets a figure sit in the text face.
- **Letterspaced caps do all the labelling.** SECTION / ROPE / Ø / MBL on R9, SIZE / WHERE / CASKS /
  DUE BACK on K7, PARTIAL / BEFORE / AFTER / METAL OFF on B4. Same size range, same tracking, same
  hairline rule beneath.
- **No photography anywhere in the set.** Every image on all three sheets is a single-weight line
  drawing of the physical object — a bell section, a cask, a rope cross-section.
- **One thin chrome bar, then straight into content.** All three put a small wordmark plus a mark
  at top left and a short caps line or nav at top right, rule underneath, and then no hero image
  on any of them.
- **The same structural unit.** A record list where each row is [drawing of the object] + [name and
  a one-line description] + [right-aligned monospace figures] + [an outlined action or a pill].

**Evidence that they are not one template:** the grounds are genuinely different decisions — warm
brown-black, dark navy, oatmeal paper — and B4 is a different kind of page altogether, an editorial
argument with a 60px serif headline, where the other two are catalogues with no display type at
all. Nothing is reused at the component level: the buttons, the rules, the header proportions and
the section spacing are drawn fresh each time.

## 2. Which two are closest, and on what?

**K7 and R9**, and the resemblance is structural rather than superficial.

Both are a thin masthead over a single table of records, and the row anatomy is the same object in
both: a line drawing of the physical thing in a leading column at roughly 110–120px, the item name
in the text face with a one-line note under it, a block of right-aligned monospace figures, and an
outlined action button parked at the far right of the row. Both then close with a small secondary
panel below the table that is currently empty — "Booked in this week / Nothing booked in yet this
week" on K7, "Your order / Nothing cut yet" on R9 — followed by a hairline rule and the disclosure
footer. Both hold their whole page inside a narrow size range with no display type. Both use the
drawing as the row's largest element.

So: **layout, row anatomy, imagery treatment and vertical spacing**. What separates them is ground
(navy versus paper) and, decisively, whether the drawing earns its size — on R9 the cross-section
*is* the comparison, on K7 the barrel repeats a word already printed beside it.

B4 is the odd one out on layout and on type, but it is not a different studio; it shares the mono,
the caps labels, the drawing treatment and the footer.

## 3. What do all three share?

A single editorial position, which is stronger than the visual recipe and is the real signature:
**each page publishes the number that does not flatter it.** B4 prints the before-tuning cents
alongside the after and the kilograms of metal removed, and adds a whole section headed "What we
will not do". K7 prints how many days late each consignment is. R9 prints minimum breaking loads
and then spends its only colour on saying those are not safe working loads and nothing here is
rated for lifting people.

Underneath that: monospaced figures and letterspaced caps doing the "this is a measured trade" work;
exactly one line-drawn diagram of the physical object as the only imagery; no stock photography and
no photography at all; a narrow accent used sparingly on dark grounds and once on the light one; and
a habit of naming the unit explicitly — cwt and qr, firkin and kilderkin, per metre — rather than
letting it be assumed.
