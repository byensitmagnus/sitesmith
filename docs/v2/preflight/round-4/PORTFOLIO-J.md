## 1. One studio applying one template, or three pieces of work?

One studio, one method, three genuinely different skins. Not one template — but not three independent
pieces either.

Evidence for one hand, in descending order of how hard it is to explain away:

- **The same footer confession, in the same position, in the same two-paragraph shape.** M2: "No such
  counter exists: the shop, the lines and the batch numbers were written for a design exercise."
  K7: "There is no such foundry and there is no such tenor. The cents figures and metal-off weights
  shown are invented for this exercise." R9: "Invented brewery, invented cellar, invented week. Casks,
  gyles and accounts here are made up; the duty rules they follow are not." Same voice, same syntax,
  same slot.
- **The same typographic device for figures.** All three bold the numbers inside a running sentence
  rather than pulling them into a spec block: "**12 mm** diameter, breaking load **3.1 t**"; "the worst
  partial is **38** cents out"; "**Late first, then due today.**"
- **All three ship a refusal.** Every page includes a state where the page declines the visitor's
  request, and in every case the refusal is written in the trade's own voice, not the framework's:
  "The minimum cut is 3 m, we cannot sell a shorter length off the coil"; "Above twelve we would rather
  ring you, put the tower in and we will call"; "Say what condition it came back in, it goes on the
  duty record." A house rule, clearly.
- **Machine identifiers get a monospace on the two pages that have them** — batch/cents figures on K7,
  "gyle 212" on R9 — and are otherwise treated identically.
- **A shared bad habit: a narrow left measure inside a wide container.** M2's rows and footer stop
  ~300px short of the container; K7's enquiry section leaves ~450x600px empty beside the form; R9's
  instruction paragraph occupies 530px of a 1230px width. All three desktop views are under-composed
  in the same direction.

Evidence against one template: the three grounds (cream paper / near-black warm / navy), the three
display faces (old-style serif / condensed grotesque caps / plain UI sans), and three different page
skeletons — an editorial product list, an editorial argument page, and a single-screen app with no
photography at all. The body sans on each looks like a different face from the same humanist shelf
rather than one shared font stack.

## 2. Which two are closest, and on what?

**M2 and K7**, on layout and imagery. Both are long editorial scroll pages: full-bleed photograph at
the top, a two-line heading over a three-or-four-line paragraph at a ~500px measure, hairline rules
marking sections, real commissioned photography with one consistent light per page, and a name-address
footer. Both use a display face plus a humanist sans plus small letterspaced caps for labels. R9 has no
hero, no photography (the header's brick texture is invisible), no editorial measure, and no display
face — it is a different kind of object.

The counter-argument is ground and mood: K7 and R9 are both dark pages with a single warm accent and
mono figures on near-black, while M2 is the only light page in the set. But that is one axis, and
K7's warm brown-black with brass reads nothing like R9's cool navy with red/amber, so I would not put
those two together.

## 3. What do all three share?

A refusal to pad. Each page shows the visitor the numbers a professional in that trade would actually
be held to — breaking load and batch, cents from equal temperament and kilograms removed, ullage in
pints against a duty record — puts the figure in bold inside a plain sentence, and then declines the
request when the request is wrong. No testimonials, no feature grids, no stock photography, no
persuasion. It is the same argument three times: here is the record, decide for yourself. That is the
studio's actual signature, more than any font or colour.
