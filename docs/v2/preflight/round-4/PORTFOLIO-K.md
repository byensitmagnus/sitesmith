## 1. One studio applying one template, or three pieces of work?

One studio, one method, three surfaces. The method is unmistakable once you have seen all
three, and it lives in the behaviour rather than the skin.

Evidence for the shared method:

- **The same page skeleton.** A full-bleed strip at the top carrying the constraint (breaking
  loads are not working loads / there is no putting it back / late first, then due today),
  then a left-aligned measure, then a repeating record row — a rope construction, a refusal
  column, a cask consignment — each with its control block sunk into a slightly tinted panel
  inside the row. M2 and R9 use structurally the same row: identity on the left, status on the
  right, the input inline beneath.
- **The same state choreography, in the same order.** Every sheet shows a refusal before a
  success: 1 m against a 3 m minimum cut; 19 bells against a range of 16; a book-in with the
  condition left unset. Every success then produces a receipt that repeats back what was
  recorded — "Your ticket" with batch numbers, "ENQUIRY SENT" with the five submitted values,
  "Booked in this week" with gyle and ullage and an Undo.
- **The same copy discipline.** UI labels are written as full sentences in the trade's idiom
  ("Cut me __ metres, which comes to", "Say what condition it came back in, it goes on the
  duty record", "We will not lift a bell out of a frame on a verbal"), and every page closes
  with a small-print paragraph in the same voice admitting the business is invented.
- **The same accent rule.** Exactly one hue is reserved for the number that matters — rust for
  price, gold for the measured figure, red/amber for lateness — and it is otherwise withheld.

Evidence against one template: the surfaces genuinely differ. M2 is warm paper, a display
serif and five real product photographs; K7 is near-black bronze, condensed caps and three
foundry photographs; R9 is cool slate, one UI sans and no photography at all. No typeface,
ground or accent hue is shared across any two of them. Nothing looks recoloured from a
sibling.

So: not one template. One recipe, cooked three ways — and the recipe is strong enough that I
can state it: lead with the constraint, make the record the interface, refuse in the client's
own words, hand back a receipt.

## 2. Which two are closest, and on what?

**K7 and R9**, on ground and structure.

- **Ground.** Both are dark pages — warm near-black bronze against cool slate — with light
  text and a single saturated accent, rather than the warm paper M2 runs on.
- **Type.** Both set numeric values in a monospace against a proportional sans and reserve the
  mono strictly for measurements and record identifiers: `4.1 kg`, `−38`, `0` in K7's partials
  table; `gyle 212`, `ullage 11 pt` in R9's rows.
- **Layout.** Both build the page from full-width horizontal bands separated by a hairline
  rule, and both put a status or summary strip across the very top before any content.
- **Interaction.** In both, the thing you act on is a form control inset in a slightly lighter
  panel inside a band, rather than a button floating on the ground.
- **The same weakness, measurably.** In both, the control that completes the job is the
  lowest-contrast element on screen — K7's empty field outlines at roughly 1.6:1 against the
  ground, R9's "Book in" at roughly 1.25:1 against its own panel. Whatever produced these two
  pages has a systematic habit of under-weighting the primary control on a dark ground.

M2 is the outlier on ground, type and imagery — but it is not a different piece of work: its
product row plus inline control block plus tinted panel is structurally the same organism as
R9's consignment row.

## 3. What do all three share?

**The refusal.** Every one of these pages shows the business saying no before it says yes, in
its own words, at the exact point of the attempt — not a generic validation string. That is
the single most distinctive thing in the set, and it is the reason none of the three reads as
a brochure: the constraint is stated on the first screen, enforced in the interaction, and
then honoured in a receipt that repeats back what was actually recorded.

Underneath that, three habits repeat: real numbers with real units treated as the content
rather than as decoration; exactly one reserved accent hue per page; and a willingness to
publish what the business will not do — the un-photographed polypropylene, the three refusal
columns, the cask that cannot be booked in without a condition. If I were told these three
came from one hand, the refusal state alone would convince me.
