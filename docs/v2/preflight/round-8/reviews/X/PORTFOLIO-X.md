## 1. One studio applying one template, or different pieces of work?

One studio, one method, three different templates. The method is unmistakable and it is not a visual
style — it is an editorial position, applied five ways on all three sheets:

- **Every key figure is drawn, and every page says so and says what the drawing does not claim.**
  W1: "Drawn, not photographed. Nobody has ever photographed these drawers." K7: "A notation, not a
  picture of a hide," and the pit count "drawn as a count, not as a survey of the yard." B4: "Nothing
  on it is computed from a height of tide, because the board holds none."
- **Every page refuses borrowed proof in writing.** K7: "Nobody is named... no list of customers on
  this page and no quotation from one." W1: "Neither association has a badge on this page, because
  neither was asked for one. They lent their names, and a name belongs in type," followed by
  "Nothing else to declare." B4 carries no proof at all, correctly, being internal.
- **Every page ends in a conventions appendix that defines its own vocabulary** — B4's "Board
  conventions", K7's "The rules / The overhang / The top edge", W1's "How to read the drawings".
- **Every page's central asset is a horizontal comparison chart with a today/now marker.** B4's tide
  ruler with a solid "now" rule and a dashed high-water rule; K7's lead-time chart with a "today,
  July 2026" rule and hatched already-in-the-pit bars; W1's spring-to-autumn rule with the red return
  arc. Same device, three subjects.
- **Mono for labels and figures, a proportional face for prose, one accent used as a state.**

Against one template: the grounds and the type are genuinely different work. B4 is a near-black
board set entirely in one monospace with no display face at all; K7 and W1 are warm paper with a
serif display. The page shapes differ too — B4 is a live board (masthead status, a log form that
writes a record), K7 is a specimen sheet that ends in an enquiry, W1 is a catalogue with a
persistent slip counter. Nobody dropped the same hero into three briefs.

## 2. Which two are closest, and on what?

K7 and W1, and it is not close. They share:

- **Ground** — the same warm paper cream, and a single oxide/brick red as the only chromatic accent
  on either page, used for the one action and for the figure the page wants believed.
- **Type** — old-style serif display over a mono for eyebrows, column heads and figures; the same
  small-caps mono label pattern (`THE NINE DRAWERS`, `AS AT JULY 2026` / `THE FIGURES, WITHOUT THE
  DRAWING`); italic serif for names within a roman line (cultivars in W1, the same treatment for the
  grades in K7's row list).
- **Imagery** — flat black ink drawings on paper, with a solid-versus-dashed encoding declared as a
  legend and then reused as the masthead mark. The two masthead marks are the same idea: a small
  drawing taken straight out of the page's own notation (a seed; a millimetre rule).
- **Layout** — a wide left column of rule-separated rows, each row being name / one-line description
  / figure / count, with a narrow right rail (K7's spec sidebar, W1's sticky slip) and a small-print
  footer.

B4 sits apart on ground and on type, though it is the same hand — its ruler is K7's lead-time chart
with the colours inverted.

## 3. What do all three share?

A refusal to illustrate. There is not one photograph, one stock image, one gradient, one isometric
figure or one floating screenshot across six sheets; every picture is a measurement drawn to a
stated scale with its own legend, and every page tells the reader what its pictures are not. The
second, rarer thing they share is that each page prints the number that damages it — 96 of 412
returns, eleven months for the goat and a window already closed, a lock out of service since 2006
kept on the board — and then writes a sentence explaining why it is printed rather than rounded.
