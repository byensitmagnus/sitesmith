## 1. One studio applying one template, or different pieces of work?

One studio, one method, three real executions — but the method is repeated closely enough that
it is fair to call some of it a recipe.

Evidence of a shared hand, in order of how hard it is to explain away:

- **The same epistemology footnote appears on all three.** B4: "the board holds no tide heights,
  so it never states the depth over a sill at a given hour" and "every figure on this board is
  either… nothing on it is computed". K7: "every figure on this page is measured, and nothing on
  it came from a camera". W1: "every picture on this page is a drawing made for it… their colours
  are not something we have written down, and we are not going to guess at them here". Three
  different subjects arriving independently at a closing note that says what the page will not
  claim is not a coincidence of category; it is one writer's tic.
- **The same structural kit.** All-caps letterspaced mono eyebrows over every section; a single
  full-width hairline as the only section divider (no boxes, no cards except one tinted panel each
  in K7 and W1); a two-column label/value table with the label in mono at the left and the value in
  the content face; figures right-aligned in a dedicated column; a form at the end whose every field
  carries a line of plain help text tied to the control.
- **The same refusal.** No photography anywhere, on any of the three. No gradient, no rounded card,
  no icon grid, no isometric figure, no screenshot at an angle. The load-bearing asset on each page
  is a drawn diagram at one declared scale with a legend.
- **The same opening move.** A sentence-length H1 that states the transaction, immediately paired
  with a compact fact table answering the practical questions, before any argument begins.

Evidence they are genuinely different pieces:

- Ground and palette are argued from the subject rather than rotated: console-dark with
  green/amber/red status on B4, parchment and bark with one oxblood on K7, paper and ink with one
  brick red on W1.
- Type differs in kind, not just family: B4 is monospace only, K7 is a high-contrast serif with mono
  figures, W1 runs three families (display serif, humanist sans body, mono labels).
- The chart ideas are three different ideas, not one chart restyled: a time ruler with an elapsed
  hairline and a remaining bar; a scale elevation of physical sections on one baseline; and a
  unit-count matrix of 412 marks. None would work in the other two briefs.
- Section rhythm differs — B4 never inverts its ground, K7 inverts exactly once for the pits, W1
  never inverts and uses a half-tone panel instead.

## 2. Which two are closest, and on what?

**K7 and W1**, and it is not close. Shared: a warm paper ground with dark ink and exactly one
saturated warm accent; an old-style serif for display; letterspaced mono eyebrows and mono figures;
ink drawings as the only imagery; near-identical hairline weights and section spacing; a
three-column footer with the same "here is what we do not claim" declaration; and a right-hand
tinted panel used once each (the lay/over note, the slip card). Put a K7 section and a W1 section
side by side with the copy removed and you would take them for the same site in two moods.

B4 is the outlier only in surface — dark ground, one monospace family. Its bones are the same kit:
same eyebrows, same hairline dividers, same label/value tables, same help-text-under-every-field
form, same closing note about what the numbers are.

## 3. What do they all share?

Every quantity on the page is drawn at a stated scale rather than described, and every page tells
you where its numbers came from and what it refuses to claim. That is the shared conviction and it
is a good one — it is why all three score well on assets and why none of them needed a photograph.

The cost is that the pages also share a shape: eyebrow, hairline, prose left, figures right, drawn
comparison in the middle, form at the end, disclaimer in the footer. On these three it reads as a
house method because each subject genuinely wanted it. On a fourth brief that did not want a
measured comparison, the same sequence would be a template, and the closing disclaimer would start
to look like a signature rather than an argument.
