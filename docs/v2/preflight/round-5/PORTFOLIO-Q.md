## 1. One studio applying one template, or three pieces of work?

One studio, one method, three genuinely different surfaces — but the method is a recipe and it
shows through.

Evidence that it is one hand, and one procedure:

- **The same page skeleton.** Claim in the first screen → a bespoke instrument that proves the
  claim (the metre stepper, the before/after partials table, the row queue) → a refusal → a
  receipt → a confession. All three, in that order.
- **The refusal is always designed content, never an error style.** B4: "The minimum cut is 3 m,
  we cannot sell a shorter length off the coil." W1: "Above twelve we would rather ring you, put
  the tower in and we will call." M2: "Say what condition it came back in, it goes on the duty
  record." Same rhetorical move — refuse, then give the trade's reason — in three different
  voices. No off-the-shelf validation copy anywhere.
- **The same completion pattern.** Every page ends its interaction with a restated receipt you
  could read aloud: Trelfall's ticket with two cuts and a total, Marrow & Kell's ENQUIRY SENT
  panel listing tower/bells/faculty/reply-to/what you said, Stalbridge's "2 × firkin from Sowerby
  Arms" row with an Undo.
- **The same confession footer**, two short lines, same cadence: "No such counter exists…",
  "There is no such foundry and there is no such tenor", "Invented brewery, invented cellar,
  invented week." Then a second line disclaiming the numbers specifically.
- **The same running-text face.** All three set their prose in the same neutral humanist sans —
  on this render the Windows UI face. B4's descriptions, W1's body copy and M2's intro paragraph
  are the same font at the same optical weight.
- **The same colour discipline.** One accent per page, reserved for the operative thing and used
  nowhere decoratively: ochre only on prices, brass only on the words that carry the argument and
  the CTA, and on M2 colour only ever for state.
- **The same failure, three times.** Each page leaves one conspicuous rectangle of empty ground
  below the fold: B4's "Not photographed" panel, W1's empty left column beside the enquiry form,
  M2's empty right half of every control row.

Evidence that they are not one template: the grounds are properly different (bone and warm white
with photography; near-black with brass; slate-navy with no photography at all), the display type
is different in kind (bookish serif, heavy caps grotesque, no display face), and the page
structures are not shared components — a two-up product grid, an editorial column with a data
table, and a single-column task queue do not come from one layout.

## 2. Which two are closest, and on what?

**W1 and M2**, on ground, on the treatment of measurement, and on accent discipline. Both sit on
a dark desaturated field. Both push every identifier and every measured quantity into a Windows
mono — W1's "4.1 kg" and "−38", M2's "gyle 212" and "ullage 11 pt" — so the machine-read numbers
are visually separated from the prose in exactly the same way. Both set their small labels as
letterspaced uppercase in a muted grey ("PARTIAL / CENTS / METAL OFF" and "Condition / Ullage,
pt" / "Late / Due today / Casks out"). Both hold to a single saturated hue against the dark, and
both put their one refusal in a rust/salmon that appears nowhere else.

The counter-pairing worth naming: **B4 and W1** are the two with real photographic direction —
one treatment, one light, consistently cropped, and used as evidence rather than decoration.
M2 has no photography at all beyond a barely-visible wash in its header, which is the single
biggest reason it is the weakest of the three.

## 3. What do all three share?

A method: find the one number or the one action a trade actually turns on — metres cut off a
coil, cents from equal temperament, casks overdue — and build the page around an instrument that
operates on it, rather than around sections. Everything else on each page exists to explain that
instrument.

Underneath that, three shared habits, two good and one not. Good: they all refuse the visitor at
least once and treat the refusal as designed copy in the client's own voice; and they all end
with a receipt rather than a "thank you". Not good: they all set their prose in the same
inherited system sans, and they all have somewhere below the fold a large rectangle where a
composition should be — which suggests the pages are assembled top-down until the content runs
out rather than composed to a whole.
