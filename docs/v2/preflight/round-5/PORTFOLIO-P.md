## 1. One studio applying one template, or three different pieces of work?

One studio, three pieces of work — a shared method, not a shared template.

Against a template, the load-bearing decisions are different in kind, not rotated:

- **Ground.** Warm bone off-white (B4), near-black warm with brass (W1), slate navy with traffic-light status (M2). None of these is the others with the hue moved.
- **Type system.** B4 runs a serif against a sans with a role rule (serif carries measured facts, sans carries description). W1 runs a condensed grotesque in caps against a humanist sans against a mono. M2 runs one interface sans against a mono. Three different answers to the same question.
- **Page shape.** B4 is a two-column product grid with a sticky ticket bar. W1 is a sequence of differently proportioned bands — full-bleed hero, text-plus-table split, three-column refusal strip, statement-plus-image, form column. M2 is a single-column list of records with no hero at all.
- **Section rhythm.** Only B4 repeats one module down the page. W1 changes shape at every section. M2 deliberately repeats because it is a list. Nobody reached for alternating bands.

For one hand, the fingerprint is in the method and it is unmistakable across all three:

- **A refusal is a designed state, in every one.** "The minimum cut is 3 m, we cannot sell a shorter length off the coil." / "Say where the faculty stands, even if that is 'I do not know'." / "Say what condition it came back in, it goes on the duty record." Same construction every time: red, adjacent to the offending control, written in the owner's voice, naming a consequence rather than a rule.
- **Data is bolded inside a running sentence rather than tabulated.** B4's "12 mm diameter, breaking load 3.1 t, stretches 14 %, £2.40 the metre." W1's "That is the whole of it. **The bright bands are the cut.**" M2's "**Late first, then due today.** Book a consignment back in from its own row…"
- **Every figure carries its unit, and the units are set apart** — metres/tonnes/percent in B4's serif, cents/kilograms in W1's mono, firkins/gallons/pints in M2's mono.
- **An em-dash stands where a value does not exist.** B4 puts one where the price would be when the cut is refused; W1 puts one in METAL OFF for the nominal that lost nothing. Same reflex, two trades.
- **Exactly one accent does the value job and is otherwise absent** — the warm brown on prices only, the brass on the foundry's own marks only, the status hues on state only.
- **The completed action reads itself back to you.** B4's ticket ("we read the ticket back to you before the knife"), W1's ENQUIRY SENT receipt ("You said: The third sounds sour."), M2's booked-in line with Undo.
- **All three close with a grey two-paragraph footer admitting the business is invented**, in the same rhetorical shape: "No such counter exists…" / "There is no such foundry and there is no such tenor." / "Invented brewery, invented cellar, invented week." That is the single clearest signature on the set.

## 2. Which two are closest, and on what?

**B4 and W1**, on three of the five axes named:

- **Layout.** Both are hero-then-sections marketing pages that end in a conversion device. M2 has no hero and no sections.
- **Imagery.** Both are built on real photography of a single object shot on a bench in one light, cropped tight, and captioned. B4: rope ends whipped on dock timber. W1: bell, boring machine, soundbow. M2 has one photograph and it is darkened to near-invisibility.
- **Spacing.** Both run an editorial measure with generous leading and a lot of air around the type. M2 is a utility list at tight rhythm.

The honest complication is that on the other two axes the pairing flips: **W1 and M2** share the ground (dark, desaturated, one saturated accent) and share the figure-setting (a mono reserved for the numbers that come off paperwork — cents and kilograms, gyles and pints). So the surface says W1 and M2 are siblings while the structure says B4 and W1 are. B4 is the outlier in appearance; M2 is the outlier in genre.

## 3. What do all three share?

Each page is organised around the one number its trade actually argues over — the metre, the cent, the day late — and each page is willing to say no.

That is a working method, and it shows in the same four places every time: the page states its constraint before it sells anything (the breaking-load warning above the fold, "there is no putting it back", "a cask coming back is a duty entry"); the number that matters is set largest and given its unit; the interface has a designed refusal sitting beside the control that caused it, written as the proprietor would say it; and the finished action is read back to the visitor as a receipt. Strip the photographs and the palettes away and that skeleton is what remains, identical in all three.

The shared weakness is also the same in all three, and it is the wide view: at 1280 each page has at least one region where content runs out and the ground does not take over — B4's empty final row and half-width footer, W1's dead left column beside the form, M2's roughly 1100px void between every pub name and its status pill. All three are better resolved at 390 than at 1440, which suggests the composition is being decided at the narrow width and stretched.
