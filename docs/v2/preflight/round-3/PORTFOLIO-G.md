## 1. One studio applying one template, or three pieces of work?

One studio, one method and one component kit — but not one template. The visual
surfaces are genuinely different and the differences are not skin-deep: B4 is a warm
cream ground with a high-contrast old-style serif and daylight macro photography on
wood; K7 is a near-black warm ground with a heavy condensed grotesque set in caps over
dark foundry photography; R9 is a cool dark slate with no display face and effectively
no photography at all. Grounds, faces, imagery and page skeletons all differ. Nobody
recolored one page three times.

What is shared is the *method*, and it is shared to the letter:

- **The label layer.** All three set every field and column label in the same small,
  muted, letterspaced uppercase sans — B4's DIAMETER / BREAKING LOAD / STRETCH / PER
  METRE / BATCH and LENGTH, METRES; K7's PARTIAL / CENTS / METAL OFF and TOWER / BELLS
  IN THE RING / FACULTY POSITION; R9's LATE / DUE TODAY / CASKS OUT and CONDITION /
  ULLAGE, PT. Same size, same tracking, same grey, on three different grounds.
- **The body sans.** Sampled side by side at 3x, B4's "Five is the whole range.",
  K7's "A bell has five partials that matter." and R9's "Book a consignment back in
  from its own row" are the same humanist sans in the same weight. It is the one
  typeface all three actually hold in common.
- **The refusal.** Every page has a refused-action state written as a trade sentence
  with a reason, never a validation stub: "The minimum cut is 3 m, we cannot sell a
  shorter length off the coil" / "Say where the faculty stands, even if that is 'I do
  not know'" / "Say what condition it came back in, it goes on the duty record."
- **The confirmation echo.** Every page answers a completed action with a small panel
  bearing a coloured left rule that reads the submitted values back — B4's "18 m of
  Kernmantle, low stretch added, £54.90, off batch KM11-2429", K7's ENQUIRY SENT
  definition list, R9's "2 x firkin back in from Sowerby Arms". Same component, three
  palettes. On B4 and R9 it duplicates the record immediately above it, which suggests
  it is one shared piece of code with one shared blind spot.
- **The disclosure footer.** All three end in the same place with the same two short
  muted paragraphs admitting the subject is invented: "No such counter exists…" /
  "There is no such foundry and there is no such tenor." / "Invented brewery, invented
  cellar, invented week."
- **One shared numeric bug.** B4's refused price cell and K7's zero-value METAL OFF
  cell both render a lone comma-or-apostrophe where a figure should be. Two different
  pages, two different data types, the same stray glyph.

## 2. Which two are closest, and on what?

**B4 and R9, on layout and on the record component.** Strip the colour and they are the
same page. Both are a vertical stack of identically proportioned rows, and the row is
the same row: a visual at the left edge (a rope macro / a drawn cask glyph), then the
identity block in the middle (name, sub-line, a small spec or status), then a control
cluster ranged right (a stepper and a price / a select and a number field), then a
single primary button at the right end of every row. Both then park a running record
underneath or beside — "Your cuts" and "Booked in this week" — with a bold item line, a
muted detail line carrying the reference number, a right-ranged Remove/Undo link, and
the confirmation echo below. Both even fail the same way: the item's detail line wraps
and orphans its last unit ("mm" on B4, "11 pt" on R9).

The rival pairing is **K7 and R9 on ground and on record type** — both are dark, both
use a monospace for reference data (K7's readings, R9's gyle numbers), both put form
controls in dark-filled rectangles with hairline borders and one saturated warm accent.
That is the pairing you see first. But it is a palette match; the B4/R9 match is the
page skeleton, which is the more telling one.

## 3. What do all three share?

A commitment to putting the trade's own record on the page and letting it do the design
work. Each page is organised around a number the business is accountable for and will
not soften: a breaking load with the batch it was measured on, a partial in cents with
the kilograms cut to get there, an ullage in pints against a gyle. In all three the
labels for those figures are the recurring typographic texture, the accent colour is
reserved for the figure that changed, the refusals are written as reasons rather than
rules, and the page admits in the footer what it does not know. It is one editorial
temperament — sober, specific, unwilling to claim — applied three times with genuinely
different visual means.
