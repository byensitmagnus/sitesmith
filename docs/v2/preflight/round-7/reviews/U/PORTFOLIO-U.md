## 1. One studio applying one template, or three different pieces of work?

One studio, three genuinely different pieces. What repeats is a method, not a layout.

The shared method, with the evidence:

- **Monospace is reserved for anything measured, in all three, without exception.** M2: £2.40, 3.1 t, TS12-2431, 184 m, "Cut from 3 m up to the 184 m on the coil." R9: −31, +18, −40 / true / +40, "cut at the crown". W1: gyle 214, Wed 15:20, 4 gal, "33 casks, 468 gallons." Prose never gets the mono; measures never get anything else.
- **Letterspaced mono caps as the eyebrow / column-label voice.** ON THE COIL and BATCH; BELL TUNING AND FRAME WORK, SUFFOLK and CROWN AT THE TOP, LIP AT THE BOTTOM; CASKS BACK and WHEN / PUB AND GYLE.
- **Identical wordmark grammar**: a small line-drawn mark, then the name in letterspaced bold caps, then a lighter descriptor set immediately beneath or beside it at a smaller size — TRELFALL & SON / ROPE AND CORDAGE, MARROW & KELL / BELLFOUNDERS, STALBRIDGE / CASK DESK. All three marks are hairline drawings of a physical section: a rope end-on, a bell wall with its cut heights, a cask.
- **Hairline rules as the only divider.** No card shadows, no rounded containers, no elevation anywhere on any of the six sheets.
- **One accent, rationed to a single action plus one status.** Brick on ADD THE CUT and the safety rule; amber on ENQUIRE / SEND and the figure 23 lb; red on the days-late numerals only. In all three the accent is deliberately withheld from the data display.
- **The same summary-sentence grammar under section heads**, mono, figures bolded against grey prose: "11 casks late back at 3 pubs · 153 gallons", "15 casks still out at 4 pubs · 198 gallons", "Booked back this week: 10 casks, 99 gallons", "Minimum cut 3 m. Whipping both ends, £1.80." Same middot separator, same construction.

What is not shared, and is the reason these are three pieces:

- **Grounds are three different families.** M2 is a kraft sand throughout with a warm near-black footer; R9 is one flat near-black from header to foot; W1 inverts slate against paper as its central device.
- **Type systems differ at the root.** M2 is the only one with a serif, and it is a real old-style with text figures carrying every sentence. R9 is proportional sans plus mono. W1 has no proportional prose face at all — condensed grotesque for names, mono for everything else.
- **Imagery models differ.** M2 has five product photographs from one bench shoot plus a letterbox hero strip. R9 has three cinematic macros, two of them captioned. W1 has no photography beyond a plaster texture in the masthead, and substitutes a repeated vector pictogram.
- **Structural models differ entirely.** M2 is a two-column transactional list with a sticky summary card. R9 is an editorial long-read that ends in a form. W1 is an operational board with no marketing structure in it — no hero, no headline promise, no CTA copy.

## 2. Which two are closest, and on what?

**M2 and W1, on ground, layout and rhythm.** Both are pale paper-toned working documents built from a repeated row: an identifier rail on the left, a photograph or pictogram, a description line, a mono value strip, and one strong action chip flush right. Both use hairline-ruled rows rather than cards. Both run a running-total sentence in mono directly under the section head. Both close with a plain-language block explaining the trade's own units and rules. Their row components are close enough that you could swap the cask row into the rope page and only the content would tell you.

The honest complication: **M2 and R9 are closest on type and purpose.** Both pair a proportional prose face with mono, both letterspace mono caps for eyebrows, both are public-facing pages ending in a conversion block, and both use real photography as the primary asset. W1 has neither a prose face nor a photograph nor a conversion.

M2 is the hinge — it shares the paper-and-rows body with W1 and the type-and-photography voice with R9. R9 and W1 are the pair with the least in common: nothing but the mono discipline, the hairlines and the rationed accent.

## 3. What do all three share?

A refusal to claim anything the page cannot evidence, built into the layout rather than added as a disclaimer.

Every one of the three gives prominent, designed space to what it will not do or cannot certify. M2 puts BREAKING LOAD IS NOT A WORKING LOAD in a red-ruled callout in the first screen, prints "We will not print a working load" as a section, and gives the uncertified line its own bordered OFF THE RACK, AND NO DATE ON IT panel with an en-dash where the batch number would be. R9 makes THREE THINGS WE WILL NOT DO the second section on the page, before any capability is described, and puts "we will not send you a quotation for one" beside the submit button. W1 states "the list is added to, never edited" above the record table and "a cask goes from this screen to the wash and nowhere else" in the footnote.

Underneath that, the same three habits: monospace for anything that was actually measured, hairlines and nothing heavier for structure, and one accent held back for a single action so the numbers can stay grey.
